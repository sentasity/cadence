Design is the stage that produces the document *you* read and approve. Where brainstorm gave you a short stub, `/c-design` grows it into the **design**: by default one document, written for a person who decides what gets built and will never read the code.

## One document is the artifact

`/c-design` turns your `00-overview.md` stub into a single document, in this order:

- **Why**: the problem and who has it.
- **What it does**: the behavior in plain English, from the point of view of whoever uses it: the normal path, what they see, what happens when things go wrong.
- **Examples and wording**: concrete situations and outcomes, and the exact words users will see where wording matters.
- **Visuals**: a diagram or mockups, only where a picture decides something.
- **Decisions**: one numbered list (`D1`, `D2`, …), each with its reason, so you can push back on the reasoning and not just the outcome.
- **Out of scope**: what this design deliberately won't do, each with a reason.

You don't approve code. You approve *this document*. That's why it's written to be read, and why it aims for about 1,500 words (`design.word_target`).

## No implementation in a design

A design never carries schemas, function signatures, file lists, or code (diagrams are fine). Those are how the thing gets built, and deciding that is the plan's job. A design that carries them turns into a plan nobody asked you to approve, and the parts you should judge get buried. Values the design must fix, like a limit or a timeout, are stated as behavior: "a key is rejected after three failed attempts."

<Aside type="note" title="Bigger features: topic documents">
When a feature genuinely has separate areas, `/c-design` offers **topic documents** (`01-…`, `02-…`), each following the same rules. The overview keeps the why, the one decisions list, and the out-of-scope list. With topic documents it also asks how to write them: all at once in parallel with a consistency sweep (the default), one at a time with a pause after each, or inline in the main session.
</Aside>

## Mockups, when the work is visual

A design with real user-facing UI/UX work can opt into one more document: `95-visual-contract`. It names the surfaces the mockups govern, indexes every mockup version, and holds the contract an implementer follows when the code and the mockup disagree.

The mockups themselves are self-contained HTML files you open in a browser, and they're versioned append-only: feedback produces a new version rather than editing the one you already looked at, so the visual history is as reviewable as the decisions list. When you approve the design, the version you approved is frozen by name, and that frozen version is what `/c-plan` hands to the implementers.

This is offered only when the design is genuinely visual. A mostly-backend design with an incidental UI touch never gets the question.

## The self-review gate before `in-review`

Before it asks you to look at anything, `/c-design` runs a **self-review pass**: no leftover placeholders, no implementation detail, within the word target, every decision in the one list and reflected consistently, every link resolving, and nothing that reads like an unresolved open question. Only after that does it flip the design to `in-review` and invite you to walk through it.

<Aside type="caution" title="Approval is yours alone">
Cadence never marks a design `approved` itself. It flips to `in-review` and waits. *You* say "approved": that explicit word is the gate between design and plan. Nothing auto-promotes.
</Aside>
